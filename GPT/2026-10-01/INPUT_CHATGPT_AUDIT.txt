# 3. THE MOST IMPORTANT STRATEGIC OBSERVATION

## The current portfolio is an all-stock portfolio with surprisingly defensive characteristics

A portfolio beta around **0.59** is very low for an investor whose stated objective is:

> maximise returns subject to moderate—not low—risk.

This does not automatically mean the portfolio is wrong.

Low-beta stocks can outperform.

But the construction method needs to demonstrate **why sacrificing market sensitivity is expected to improve geometric wealth**, rather than simply reduce volatility.

At present, much of the low-beta profile arises mechanically from:

- inverse-volatility weighting;
- sector limits;
- preference for fair/cheap current valuation;
- a large eligible universe of mature cash-generative companies;
- rejection of several high-growth mega-cap names because current prices require more growth than the analysts' base cases.

This may produce a sensible portfolio.

But it may also produce a portfolio that is inadvertently optimised for **comfort rather than return**.

That distinction is central.

For the stated mandate, the appropriate question is not:

> Which portfolio is least volatile?

It is:

> Which portfolio has the highest credible long-term compound-return expectation while keeping concentration, permanent-loss risk, factor exposure and drawdown within a moderate-risk budget?

Those are different optimisation problems.

# 6. THE BIGGEST CURRENT PROBLEM: PORTFOLIO ORDER DEPENDENCE

This is now the most important issue in the entire investment system.

The project currently has roughly:

- 214 eligible companies
- 20 available slots.

The final 20 are selected through a lexicographic ordering based broadly on:

1. conviction;
2. margin of safety / implied growth versus base;
3. base-case return;
4. constraints and tie-breaks.

The project tested alternative orderings.

Only **6 of 20 holdings survive all tested orderings**.

That is extremely informative.

It says that for roughly 70% of the portfolio, the evidence does not strongly distinguish the selected stock from reasonable alternatives.

This does not mean the selected stocks are bad.

It means the methodology's **resolution is weaker than its output precision**.

A system that says:

> "These exactly 20 names are the portfolio"

looks more certain than the evidence supports.

## Best-in-class fix

Stop using a sequential walk-down ranking as the final portfolio constructor.

Instead:

> Evaluate the entire eligible universe simultaneously.

For every eligible company estimate:

- expected annualised return;
- uncertainty around that return;
- bear-case permanent-loss risk;
- cyclicality;
- balance-sheet risk;
- quality/durability;
- correlation with other candidates;
- factor exposures;
- valuation sensitivity;
- catalyst path;
- thesis confidence;
- forecast reliability.

Then solve for the portfolio.

This changes the question from:

> Who comes first in the ordered list?

to:

> Which combination of securities produces the strongest expected portfolio outcome?

That is a major upgrade.

The project has audit scores such as:

It can become Goodhart's Law:

> When a measure becomes the target, it stops being a good measure.

An audit score can reward:

- additional caveats;
- more citations;
- more documentation;
- more checks;
- more conservative assumptions;
- cleaner formatting.

Those may increase process quality.

They do not necessarily increase investment returns.

The objective function for the next version should be:

> **Expected long-term geometric wealth after realistic costs and taxes, constrained by moderate-risk limits and protected against permanent impairment.**

Audit scores should become **gates**, not objectives.

For example:

- data integrity must pass;
- valuation arithmetic must pass;
- source traceability must pass;
- portfolio constraints must pass;
- unresolved critical facts must pass.

Once those are passed, the portfolio should be selected for investment merit—not for the audit score.

# 8. THE CURRENT WEIGHTING SYSTEM IS NOT ALIGNED ENOUGH WITH THE MANDATE

Current weights are driven heavily by inverse volatility.

This is why WEC can reach roughly **8.3%**, while some potentially higher-return businesses receive smaller allocations.

Inverse volatility is a useful risk-control technique.

But it answers:

> How do I allocate less to volatile securities?

It does not answer:

> Where is my highest expected reward for the risk?

For someone explicitly seeking **moderate risk**, inverse-volatility should not be the primary determinant of position size.

## Better sizing concept

Position size should depend on four things:

### 1. Expected return

How attractive is the security under a conservatively normalised scenario?

### 2. Confidence / uncertainty

How wide is the range of plausible intrinsic outcomes?

### 3. Downside / permanent-loss risk

How bad can the fundamental outcome become?

### 4. Portfolio covariance

How much incremental risk does the security add to the entire portfolio?

A simplified conceptual rule could be:

\[
\text{Desired weight}
\propto
\frac{\text{shrunk expected excess return} \times \text{confidence}}
{\text{incremental portfolio risk}}
\]

subject to:

- maximum position;
- sector exposure;
- liquidity;
- fundamental concentration;
- downside risk.

Do **not** mechanically implement this exact formula without calibration.

The point is structural:

> expected return belongs in sizing.

The current inverse-volatility implementation gives volatility a very large role while expected return primarily affects selection.

That is not ideal for the stated objective.

# 27. SHRINK ANALYST EXPECTED RETURNS

This is extremely important.

An analyst model saying:

> expected return = 18%

should not automatically feed 18% into portfolio optimisation.

Humans and LLMs overestimate forecast precision.

Use shrinkage:

\[
\mu_{\text{usable}}
=
\lambda \mu_{\text{analyst}}
+
(1-\lambda)\mu_{\text{prior}}
\]

where λ depends on forecast quality.

Examples:

- highly predictable company + multiple methods agree → higher λ;
- cyclical turnaround + wide scenario spread → lower λ;
- questionable data → very low λ.

This prevents the optimiser from loading aggressively into the most optimistic model.
